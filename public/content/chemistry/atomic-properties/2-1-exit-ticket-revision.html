Question 11 | Interpreting the gold-foil experiment3 marks

When alpha particles were fired at thin gold foil, most passed nearly straight through; some were deflected, and very few were scattered backward.

(a) Which observation suggests that an atom is mostly empty space? (1 mark)
(b) Which observation supports a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus? Explain briefly. (2 marks)
Solution and reasoning
(a) Most alpha particles passed nearly straight through the foil, supporting the conclusion that most atomic volume is empty space; most particles did not pass close enough to the nucleus to be strongly deflected.
(b) The small number scattered through large angles or backward indicates that positive charge and almost all atomic mass are concentrated in a tiny region that repels the positive alpha particles.
Question 12 | Energy of a photon3 marks

A spectral line has a wavelength of 410 nm. Calculate (a) its frequency and (b) the energy of one photon. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s and h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

(a) Frequency ν (1 mark)
(b) Photon energy E (2 marks)
Solution and reasoning
Convert first: λ = 410 × 10⁻⁹ m = 4.10 × 10⁻⁷ m.
(a) ν = c/λ = (3.00 × 10⁸)/(4.10 × 10⁻⁷) = 7.32 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
(b) E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(7.32 × 10¹⁴) = 4.85 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon.

For H, E₂ = −3.40 eV and E₃ = −1.51 eV. An electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2. Calculate the photon energy in eV and its wavelength in nm. State whether light is absorbed or emitted. Take 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

(a) Identify the type of transition. (1 mark)
(b) Find the photon energy in eV. (1 mark)
(c) Calculate the wavelength in nm. (2 marks)
Solution and reasoning
(a) Light is emitted: the atom loses energy as the electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2.
(b) E_photon = |E₂ − E₃| = |−3.40 − (−1.51)| = 1.89 eV.
(c) Keep intermediate digits: E = 1.89 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.02778 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Choose λ = hc/E and substitute: λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(3.02778 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 6.5652… × 10⁻⁷ m = 656.52… nm ≈ 657 nm (3 significant figures).

2.1 Atomic models | Evidence that changed our view

CHEMISTRY · M.4 · Pre-quiz review · 1/2

Models describe structures too small to observe directly. They are inferred from experiments and revised when new evidence appears. Memorising only a model’s shape or the scientist’s name is not enough.

ModelEvidence and key ideaLimitation / reason for revision
DaltonAtoms are the units of elements; compounds contain simple whole-number ratios of atoms.Subatomic particles and isotopes show that atoms are divisible and same-element atoms need not have identical masses.
ThomsonCathode rays bend toward the positive plate and show the same e/m across tested conditions: electrons are present in atoms.A diffuse positive charge cannot explain rare, very large-angle alpha scattering.
RutherfordMost alpha particles pass straight through gold foil; very few deflect strongly: mostly empty space with a small positive nucleus.The classical model does not explain atomic stability or discrete line spectra.
BohrHydrogen produces line spectra: electrons have quantised energy levels and absorb or emit photons during transitions.Works well for hydrogen but is limited for multi-electron atoms.
Electron cloudQuantum orbitals express the probability of finding an electron in a region.The cloud is not a fixed circular path followed by an electron.

Do not confuse the evidence: “most pass straight through” suggests empty space, whereas “a few scatter through large angles/backward” suggests a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.

Spectra, photon energy and exam connections

CHEMISTRY · M.4 · Pre-quiz review · 2/2

Spectra and energy transitions

A hot incandescent filament produces a continuous spectrum. Excited low-pressure gases produce characteristic line spectra because electrons have discrete energy levels. A downward electronic transition emits a photon; a higher transition requires absorption.

Downward transition → emission / Upward transition → absorption

Essential equations

c = λνE = hνE = hc/λ = |ΔE|

c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s (speed of light in vacuum); h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck constant); λ in m; ν in Hz; 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m

Choose the equation, then substitute

For λ = 410 nm: λ = 410 × 10⁻⁹ m; ν = c/λ = 7.32 × 10¹⁴ Hz; E = hc/λ = 4.85 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon.

Exam-ready connections

  1. Follow evidence to conclusion: identify exactly which observation supports which model.
  2. An orbital is a region of probability, not a Bohr orbit; a denser cloud does not mean more electrons.
  3. Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → greater photon energy; E ∝ 1/λ.
  4. For H: Lyman transitions end at n = 1 (UV), Balmer at n = 2 (visible members), Paschen at n = 3 (IR). This is a useful spectrum extension.
  5. For a transition use |E_final − E_initial| for positive photon energy, and state whether absorption or emission occurs. Keep intermediate digits and round only the final result; Question 13 uses rounded energy-level data.
