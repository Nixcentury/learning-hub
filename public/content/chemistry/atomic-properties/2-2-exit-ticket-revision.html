An element is identified by its number of (1)protons in the nucleus; each has a relative charge of +1. The (2)neutrons have no electric charge. These two particles are found together in the (3)nucleus, which contains almost all the mass of an atom.

The negatively charged particles found around the nucleus are (4)electrons, whose mass is much smaller than that of protons. An atom is (5)electrically neutral when its proton and electron counts are equal. Otherwise, it is an ion.

Question 12 | Chlorine isotope and ion3 marks

Consider 3717Cl− and the neutral atom 3517Cl. Compare their particle counts and isotope relationship.

(a) How many neutrons are in Cl-37⁻? (1 mark)
(b) How many electrons are in Cl-37⁻? (1 mark)

A nucleus contains protons and usually neutrons; hydrogen-1 has no neutron. Electrons occupy the surrounding region. Proton number identifies the element, isotopes differ in neutron number, and p − e determines the signed net charge.

Three main subatomic particles

ParticleRelative chargeApproximate massLocation
Proton (p)+1≈ 1 uNucleus
Neutron (n)0≈ 1 uNucleus
Electron (e)−1≈ 5.49 × 10⁻⁴ uOutside nucleus

How to read a nuclide symbol

AZXq

A = mass number = p + n; Z = atomic number = p; N = A − Z. Ion charge q = p − e (in elementary-charge units), hence e = Z − q with the sign included: 2+ means q = +2 and 1− means q = −1.

Upper left: mass number A
Lower left: atomic number Z
Upper right: charge q (if any)

What changes when an ion forms?

For example, 2713Al has 13 p, 14 n and 13 e; 2713Al3+ still has 13 p and 14 n but only 10 e after losing three electrons. Electron transfer changes neither Z nor A.

Isotopes: the same element, different neutrons

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with equal Z but different N, hence different A. Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 all contain 6 protons, but have 6, 7 and 8 neutrons, respectively. Neutral isotopes have essentially the same electron structure and usually similar chemistry, though mass-dependent properties can differ.

Essential relationships and reasoning order

A = Z + NN = A − Ze = Z − q

A = mass number (dimensionless count); Z = atomic number; N = neutron count; p = protons; e = electrons; q = signed ionic charge in units of elementary charge

Reverse example (choose → substitute)

X²⁺ has 18 e and 22 n ⇒ p = 18 + 2 = 20 ⇒ Z = 20 (Ca); A = p + n = 20 + 22 = 42; the ion is 4220Ca2+ and neutral Ca has 20 e.

High-value A-Level connections (explicit extensions)

  1. For ions: positive charge means electrons were lost; negative charge means electrons were gained. Proton count stays unchanged.
  2. Use a p/n/e table to distinguish isotopes from differently charged ions of the same nuclide.
  3. Isobars*: distinct elements with equal A; isotones*: distinct elements with equal N. Neither is the definition of isotopes.
  4. The decimal atomic mass shown on a periodic table is not an isotope’s integer mass number A; weighted-average mass calculations are taught in the relevant later topic.
  5. * Extensions for interpreting data, not extra mandatory IPST §2.2 learning outcomes.
