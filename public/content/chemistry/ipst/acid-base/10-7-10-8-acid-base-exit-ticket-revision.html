ก. จงเขียนสมการไอออน ตั้งนิพจน์ K และอธิบายว่าผลิตภัณฑ์คืออะไร

ผลิตภัณฑ์คือไอออนของแอมโมเนียมแอซีเตต แบบเรียนอาจใช้ NH₄OH แทนแอมโมเนียในน้ำ สมการไอออนสุทธิใช้ NH₃(aq)

\(K=\dfrac{[\mathrm{A^-}][\mathrm{BH^+}]}{[\mathrm{HA}][\mathrm B]}=\dfrac{K_a(\mathrm{HA})}{K_a(\mathrm{BH^+})}=\dfrac{K_a(\mathrm{HA})K_b(\mathrm B)}{K_w}\)

Example 1 · Titration and concentration

Example data. Pipette 10.00 mL of NaOH per trial. Add 0.2000 mol/L HCl until BTB stays green. Burette readings are 0.20 → 21.25 mL in trial 1 and 1.10 → 22.25 mL in trial 2.

\(n\,(\mathrm{mmol})=C\,(\mathrm{mmol/mL})\,V\,(\mathrm{mL})\)

(1)

a. Calculate the mean volume of HCl delivered.

Find the volume delivered in each trial, then average.

\[\begin{aligned}V_1 &= (21.25-0.20)\,\mathrm{mL}=21.05\,\mathrm{mL} \\ V_2 &= (22.25-1.10)\,\mathrm{mL}=21.15\,\mathrm{mL} \\ \overline V &= \frac{21.05+21.15}{2}\,\mathrm{mL}=21.10\,\mathrm{mL}\end{aligned}\]

b. Find the amount of NaOH in mmol and its original concentration.

Use the endpoint to estimate equivalence and apply (1) in mmol.

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{HCl+NaOH\longrightarrow NaCl+H_2O} \\ n(\mathrm{HCl}) &= (21.10\,\mathrm{mL})\frac{0.2000\,\mathrm{mmol}}{\mathrm{mL}} =4.220\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ n(\mathrm{NaOH}) &\approx n(\mathrm{HCl})=4.220\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ C(\mathrm{NaOH}) &\approx \frac{4.220\,\mathrm{mmol}}{10.00\,\mathrm{mL}} =0.4220\,\mathrm{mol/L}\end{aligned}\]

The mL units cancel when finding mmol. One mmol/mL equals one mol/L.

c. What does the persistent green colour signal and how does it relate to equivalence? Then calculate the concentration from your own readings.

The observed colour signals the endpoint. Equivalence occurs when acid and base have reacted in the exact stoichiometric ratio. Approximate equivalence using the endpoint, then use your mean volume and actual HCl concentration in (1).

Extend Example 1 · A 1:2 ratio and reverse unit conversions

Transfer 10.00 mL of Ba(OH)₂ from a 100.00 mL volumetric flask. Add 15.00 mL of water, then titrate with 0.1000 mol/L HCl. Equivalence requires 20.00 mL of HCl. The molar mass of Ba(OH)₂ is 171.3 g/mol.

d. Find the original concentration and the total mass of solute in the flask.

Balance the equation and use the aliquot volume before dilution.

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2+2HCl\longrightarrow BaCl_2+2H_2O} \\ n(\mathrm{HCl}) &= (20.00\,\mathrm{mL})(0.1000\,\mathrm{mmol/mL}) =2.000\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ n_{\mathrm{aliquot}}(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}) &= \frac{2.000}{2}\,\mathrm{mmol} =1.000\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ C &= \frac{1.000\,\mathrm{mmol}}{10.00\,\mathrm{mL}} =0.1000\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ n_{\mathrm{flask}} &= (100.00\,\mathrm{mL})(0.1000\,\mathrm{mmol/mL}) =10.00\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ m &= (10.00\,\mathrm{mmol})(0.1713\,\mathrm{g/mmol})=1.713\,\mathrm g\end{aligned}\]

The added water dilutes the aliquot. The amount transferred stays the same.

e. The only acid-consuming ingredient in a 0.500 g tablet is Mg(OH)₂. It reacts exactly with 25.00 mL of 0.200 mol/L HCl. Find the mass percentage of Mg(OH)₂ (58.32 g/mol).

Use the balanced coefficients to convert mmol of HCl to mmol of Mg(OH)₂.

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2+2HCl\longrightarrow MgCl_2+2H_2O} \\ n(\mathrm{HCl}) &= (25.00\,\mathrm{mL})(0.200\,\mathrm{mmol/mL}) =5.00\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ n(\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2}) &= \frac{5.00}{2}\,\mathrm{mmol}=2.50\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ m &= (2.50\,\mathrm{mmol})(0.05832\,\mathrm{g/mmol})=0.1458\,\mathrm g \\ \%\,m/m &= \frac{0.1458\,\mathrm g}{0.500\,\mathrm g}\times100\%\approx29.2\%\end{aligned}\]

Example 2 · Strong acid and strong base

Split 0.100 mol/L HCl into 25.00 mL portions. Add 20.00, 25.00 and 30.00 mL of 0.100 mol/L NaOH, respectively. Throughout the worksheet assume additive volumes and use Kw = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C.

\(\mathrm{pH}=-\log[\mathrm{H_3O^+}],\quad \mathrm{pOH}=-\log[\mathrm{OH^-}],\quad \mathrm{pH}+\mathrm{pOH}=14.00\)

(2)

For 20.00 mL of NaOH, identify the excess species, salt and pH.

Use the HCl left in row F.

\[\begin{aligned}n_{\mathrm F}(\mathrm{HCl}) &= (2.50-2.00)\,\mathrm{mmol}=0.50\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ [\mathrm{H_3O^+}] &\approx \frac{0.50\,\mathrm{mmol}}{45.00\,\mathrm{mL}}=1.11\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \mathrm{pH} &= -\log(1.11\times10^{-2})\approx1.95\end{aligned}\]

The salt is NaCl.

For 25.00 mL of NaOH, identify the excess species, salt and pH.

Acid and base are fully consumed at equivalence, forming NaCl.

\[\begin{aligned}n(\mathrm{HCl}) &= n(\mathrm{NaOH})=2.50\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ [\mathrm{H_3O^+}] &= \sqrt{K_w}=1.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \mathrm{pH} &= 7.00\end{aligned}\]

NaCl ions do not appreciably hydrolyse, so the solution is neutral at 25 °C.

For 30.00 mL of NaOH, identify the excess species, salt and pH.

Use the NaOH left in row F.

\[\begin{aligned}n_{\mathrm F}(\mathrm{OH^-}) &= (3.00-2.50)\,\mathrm{mmol}=0.50\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ [\mathrm{OH^-}] &\approx \frac{0.50\,\mathrm{mmol}}{55.00\,\mathrm{mL}}=9.09\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \mathrm{pH} &= 14.00+\log(9.09\times10^{-3})\approx11.96\end{aligned}\]

The salt is NaCl. Excess OH⁻ controls the pH.

Example 4 · Weak base and strong acid

Use 25.00 mL portions of 0.100 mol/L NH₃. Add 10.00, 25.00 and 30.00 mL of 0.100 mol/L HCl. Use Kb(NH₃) = 1.8 × 10−5.

\(\mathrm{NH_3+H_3O^+\longrightarrow NH_4^++H_2O}\)

\(K_b=\dfrac{x(C_{\mathrm{BH^+}}+x)}{C_{\mathrm B}-x},\qquad x=[\mathrm{OH^-}]\)

(5)

a. Which species remain in the 10.00 mL flask, and why is it a buffer? Calculate the pH.

Row F contains 1.50 mmol NH₃ and 1.00 mmol NH₄⁺ in 35.00 mL. Use ICE and (5).

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{NH_3+H_2O\rightleftharpoons NH_4^++OH^-} \\ K_b &= \frac{x(1.00/35.00+x)}{1.50/35.00-x} \\ x &\approx (1.8\times10^{-5})\frac{1.50}{1.00} =2.70\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \frac{x}{1.00/35.00}\times100\% &\approx0.095\% \\ \mathrm{pH} &=14.00+\log(2.70\times10^{-5})\approx9.43\end{aligned}\]

NH₃ and NH₄⁺ from ammonium chloride are both present, forming a basic buffer.

b. What salt forms in the 25.00 mL flask, which ion hydrolyses, and what is the pH?

Equivalence gives ammonium chloride. Set up ICE for NH₄⁺.

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{NH_4^++H_2O\rightleftharpoons NH_3+H_3O^+} \\ C_0 &= \frac{2.50\,\mathrm{mmol}}{50.00\,\mathrm{mL}} =0.0500\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ K_a &= \frac{10^{-14}}{1.8\times10^{-5}}\approx5.56\times10^{-10} \\ K_a &= \frac{x^2}{0.0500-x}\approx \frac{x^2}{0.0500} \\ x &= [\mathrm{H_3O^+}]\approx\sqrt{(0.0500)(5.56\times10^{-10})} =5.27\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \frac{x}{0.0500}\times100\% &\approx0.011\% \\ \mathrm{pH} &= -\log(5.27\times10^{-6})\approx5.28\end{aligned}\]

NH₄⁺ hydrolysis produces H₃O⁺, so the solution is acidic.

c. What controls the pH of the 30.00 mL flask, and what is the pH?

Use the excess H₃O⁺ from HCl after ICF.

\[\begin{aligned}n_{\mathrm{excess}}(\mathrm{H_3O^+}) &= (3.00-2.50)\,\mathrm{mmol}=0.50\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ [\mathrm{H_3O^+}] &\approx \frac{0.50\,\mathrm{mmol}}{55.00\,\mathrm{mL}} =9.09\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \mathrm{pH} &= -\log(9.09\times10^{-3})\approx2.04\end{aligned}\]

Excess acid controls the pH and suppresses NH₄⁺ hydrolysis.

Example 5 · Weak acid and weak base

Mix 25.00 mL each of 0.100 mol/L CH₃COOH and 0.100 mol/L NH₃. Use the same equilibrium constants.

a. Write the ionic equation and the expression for K. Identify the product.

Add HA dissociation to the reverse of BH⁺ dissociation.

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{CH_3COOH+NH_3(aq)\rightleftharpoons CH_3COO^-+NH_4^+} \\ K &= \frac{[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}][\mathrm{NH_4^+}]}{[\mathrm{CH_3COOH}][\mathrm{NH_3}]} \\ &\mathrm{HA+H_2O\rightleftharpoons A^-+H_3O^+}\qquad K_a(\mathrm{HA}) \\ &\mathrm{B+H_3O^+\rightleftharpoons BH^++H_2O}\qquad \frac{1}{K_a(\mathrm{BH^+})} \\ &\mathrm{HA+B\rightleftharpoons A^-+BH^+} \\ K &= \frac{K_a(\mathrm{HA})}{K_a(\mathrm{BH^+})}=\frac{K_a(\mathrm{HA})K_b(\mathrm B)}{K_w}\end{aligned}\]

The products are ammonium acetate ions. Textbooks may use NH₄OH for aqueous ammonia. The net ionic equation uses NH₃(aq).

\(K=\dfrac{[\mathrm{A^-}][\mathrm{BH^+}]}{[\mathrm{HA}][\mathrm B]}=\dfrac{K_a(\mathrm{HA})}{K_a(\mathrm{BH^+})}=\dfrac{K_a(\mathrm{HA})K_b(\mathrm B)}{K_w}\)

(6)

b. Calculate K, then explain the pH of the mixture using the equilibria of both ions.

Use (6) to find K, then compare the hydrolysis of the two ions.

\[\begin{aligned}K &= \frac{(1.8\times10^{-5})(1.8\times10^{-5})}{1.0\times10^{-14}} =3.24\times10^4 \\ K_a(\mathrm{NH_4^+}) &= \frac{K_w}{K_b(\mathrm{NH_3})}\approx5.56\times10^{-10} \\ K_b(\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}) &= \frac{K_w}{K_a(\mathrm{CH_3COOH})}\approx5.56\times10^{-10}\end{aligned}\]

The large K favours products. The two ions form in equal amounts and have equal hydrolysis constants, so the pH is close to 7.00.

\([\mathrm{H_3O^+}]^2=K_a(\mathrm{HA})K_a(\mathrm{BH^+})\,\dfrac{[\mathrm{HA}][\mathrm{BH^+}]}{[\mathrm{A^-}][\mathrm{B}]}\approx K_a(\mathrm{HA})K_a(\mathrm{BH^+})\)

(7)

Multiply the Kₐ expressions for HA and BH⁺. With equal initial analytical amounts and negligible amounts of water ions, use [HA] ≈ [B] and [A⁻] ≈ [BH⁺] in (7). Here [H₃O⁺] ≈ √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 5.56 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol/L.

c. At equal concentrations, rank CH₃COONa, NH₄Cl and CH₃COONH₄ by decreasing pH, giving a reason.

CH₃COONa > CH₃COONH₄ > NH₄Cl. Acetate produces OH⁻ and ammonium produces H₃O⁺. For the salt with both, the two ion constants are similar, giving a nearly neutral solution.

A contains dilute NaCl. B contains 0.600 mmol H₂PO₄⁻ and 0.400 mmol HPO₄²⁻. Each starts at 20.00 mL and receives a total of 0.50 mL of 0.100 mol/L HCl. Use Ka2 = 6.2 × 10−8.

\[\begin{aligned}|\Delta\mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm A}| &= |2.61-7.00|=4.39 \\ |\Delta\mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm B}| &= |6.94-7.03|=0.09\end{aligned}\]

B changes less in this example. Use the measurements from your own group.

b. Complete ICF for B after adding HCl. Use ICE to calculate its pH before and after the addition, then compare with your readings.

Do ICF in mmol, then use the total volume to convert to mol/L for ICE.

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}+H_3O^+\longrightarrow H_2PO_4^-+H_2O} \\ n(\mathrm{HCl}) &= (0.50\,\mathrm{mL})(0.100\,\mathrm{mmol/mL})=0.050\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ n_{\mathrm F}(\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}}) &= (0.400-0.050)\,\mathrm{mmol}=0.350\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ n_{\mathrm F}(\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}) &= (0.600+0.050)\,\mathrm{mmol}=0.650\,\mathrm{mmol} \\ &\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-+H_2O\rightleftharpoons H_3O^++HPO_4^{2-}} \\ K_{a2} &= \frac{x(C_{\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}}}+x)}{C_{\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}}-x} \\ x_1 &\approx (6.2\times10^{-8})\frac{0.600/20.00}{0.400/20.00} =9.30\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ x_2 &\approx (6.2\times10^{-8})\frac{0.650/20.50}{0.350/20.50} \approx1.15\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol/L} \\ \frac{x_2}{0.350/20.50}\times100\% &\approx0.00067\% \\ \mathrm{pH}_1 &= -\log(x_1)\approx7.03 \\ \mathrm{pH}_2 &= -\log(x_2)\approx6.94 \\ \Delta\mathrm{pH} &\approx6.94-7.03=-0.09\end{aligned}\]

Compare with the measured change in B. HPO₄²⁻ remains after taking up acid, so pH changes only slightly.

c. If B receives 0.400 mmol of HCl in total, how does the original buffer pair change?

In the first ICF step HPO₄²⁻ is just consumed and more H₂PO₄⁻ is formed. The original pair loses much of its capacity to take up added acid. To find the new pH, consider the equilibria of H₂PO₄⁻, which can act as either an acid or a base.
